Because of the pivotal role played by heterogeneous catalysis in modern society, it is highly desirable to improve efficiency and limit environmental impact of such processes. A fundamental understanding of the underlying mechanism and kinetics is essential to achieving these goals. A catalytic process typically consists of multiple elemental steps and the overall reaction rate is determined by a network of micro-kinetic equations. A major challenge in understanding catalysis is the lack of theoretical methods for accurately predicting the rates of elementary surface processes<sup>1–4</sup> . Unlike gas phase reactions, interfacial processes are often complicated by additional energy 

dissipation channels and inter-adsorbate interactions, which make such endeavors significantly more challenging. 

Recent years have witnessed rapid progress in the experimental measurement of reaction rates for surface processes. For example, Wodtke and coworkers developed a velocity resolved kinetics (VRK) method based on velocity map imaging<sup>5</sup> to accurately measure rate coefficients of several representative surface reactions<sup>6–12</sup> . These measurements provide valuable benchmarks for testing rate theories for surface reactions<sup>13</sup> . 

Theoretically, the rate coefficient is typically estimated using the Eyring’s transition state theory (TST)<sup>14,15</sup> . TST is based on the premise

that the thermal rate for a reaction is determined by the Boltzmann population at the activated complex, which can be calculated using statistical mechanics. Additionally, it is assumed in the TST that there exist a dividing surface separating reactants and products, and trajectories crossing the dividing surface toward the product do not recross<sup>16</sup> . To reduce errors introduced by neglecting recrossing, the dividing surface can be chosen variationally to minimize the TST rate coefficient<sup>17</sup> . Furthermore, tunneling is not included in the standard TST framework and must be accounted for through various approximate methods<sup>17</sup> . In most TST calculations, the harmonic approximation (HA) is employed to compute partition functions, requiring only the energetics and harmonic frequencies of the saddle point and reactant state along the minimum energy path on the potential energy surface (PES). For surface processes, such information can be readily obtained from density functional theory (DFT) calculations<sup>1,2</sup> . While very efficient, harmonic TST neglects anharmonicity, which can substantially affect entropy. Such effects are especially pronounced for soft modes, such as adsorbate diffusion, where surface corrugation renders the PES particularly anharmonic<sup>18–20</sup> . Obviously, these approximations of TST may lead to significant errors in the calculated rate coefficient. Indeed, for CO oxidation on Pt(111), the harmonic TST rate coefficients based on DFT data are 20–100 times larger than measured ones<sup>7</sup> . This large discrepancy underscores the need for more accurate rate theories. 

Beyond these TST shortcomings, the calculated rate coefficient may also be affected by inaccuracies in the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) density functionals commonly used in DFT calculations<sup>21</sup> , which can affect both the enthalpic and entropic contributions. To delineate the origin of errors in rate calculations, it is thus important to compare experimental data with accurate rate theory, which can provide a quantitative assessment of the accuracy of the functional used in generating the PES. This complements the comparison between different rate calculations on the same PES, which helps to identify the underlying approximations in rate models. 

The HA in TST can be avoided by computing the free-energy barrier of an elementary process by molecular dynamics (MD). Indeed, such calculations have been reported for several surface processes using either ab initio MD (AIMD) or MD on machine learned PESs<sup>22–31</sup> , with various enhanced sampling techniques<sup>32–34</sup> . However, these studies typically involve a single reaction channel where one reaction coordinate (RC) is often sufficient. With few exceptions<sup>35</sup> , the influence of dynamical recrossing is ignored. Rigorously speaking, rate coefficients obtained by simply coupling Eyring’s TST equation with the MDbased free energy barrier are unjustified<sup>36</sup> . 

A more general and rigorous approach for computing rate coefficients of reactions is the reactive flux theory (RFT), which expresses the rate coefficient in terms of a flux-side correlation function<sup>37,38</sup> . Further factorization via the Bennett-Chandler method<sup>39,40</sup> results in two terms, a TST rate coefficient in the short-time limit and a transmission coefficient accounting for the recrossing effects, both of which can be accurately determined by MD<sup>41</sup> . In the gas phase, RFT has been combined with the path-integral-based ring polymer molecular dynamics (RPMD) method<sup>42</sup> to incorporate nuclear quantum effects such as tunneling and zero-point energy<sup>43–47</sup> . RPMD replaces a quantum particle with a necklace of harmonically connected beads, and as a result, the dynamics can be followed by the classical motion of the ring polymer. This classical-quantum isomorphism enables the use of Newton’s equation in simulating the dynamics, thus making it numerically efficient. Using ab initio PESs, the RPMD-based RFT has been validated by comparing with exact quantum dynamics calculations for several gaseous bimolecular reactions<sup>48</sup> . Applications of the RPMD-based RFT to surface diffusion have also been reported<sup>49,50</sup> . Very recently, we have presented RPMD rate calculations for NO desorption from Pd(111)<sup>51</sup> and for H2 dissociation/recombination on multiple metal surfaces<sup>52–54</sup> . Agreement with experimental results is quite satisfactory. 

Nevertheless, these RFT calculations so far have been restricted to processes involving a single specific RC, in which the umbrella sampling method has been used to evaluate the one-dimensional freeenergy profile<sup>55</sup> . On surfaces, several reaction channels may coexist with similar barriers, and these competing processes might be temporally and spatially inseparable. In such cases, more than one RC is needed in rate coefficient calculations, and a more efficient enhanced sampling method is necessary. We note in passing that alternatives, such as the Hill relation<sup>56</sup> can also be used to accurately calculate reaction rates<sup>57</sup> . 

In this work, we extend the RFT calculations within a general framework to surface reactions of any order involving multiple RCs. The RFT is integrated with a well-tempered metadynamics (WTMetaD)<sup>58</sup> method to treat multiple RCs and applied to CO oxidation on Pt(111), which is not only an important reaction involved in many industrially catalytic processes<sup>59</sup> but also a prototype for testing theories/experiments<sup>7,60,61</sup> . To make this approach computationally tractable, we develop a high-dimensional neural network PES that achieves accuracy comparable to DFT while being orders of magnitude more efficient. With no adjustable parameters, the RFT-calculated rate coefficients for both the CO oxidation and CO desorption processes are in reasonably good agreement with the experiment. When benchmarked against this accurate rate theory, approximate TST methods are found to exhibit significant, channel-dependent errors. In addition, dynamic recrossing effects are uncovered in both reactions, arising from distinct physical mechanisms and highlighting the importance of dynamical effects in surface reaction kinetics. These theoretical results shed valuable light on the microscopic details of surface processes. 

## Results 

As detailed in the Methods section, the RFT used in this work overcomes the limitations of conventional Eyring’s TST for surface reactions in several aspects. First, the multi-dimensional free energy surface (FES) is mapped out by WT-MetaD, which fully accounts for the anharmonic effect in the potential of mean force (PMF) along the RC. Second, surface atoms are allowed to move, and their soft vibrational modes are included in FES calculations. Third, the transmission coefficient (κ) is determined by launching numerous trajectories at the dividing surface towards both the reactant and product sides and counting those returned ones with opposite velocities. Apparently, such extensive MD simulations are far beyond the limit of AIMD, and constructing an analytical PES becomes essential. To this end, the embedded atom neural network (EANN) method<sup>62–64</sup> is used to fit thousands of DFT data points to yield a high-dimensional PES with firstprinciples accuracy, as described in Methods and Supplementary Information (SI). 

### Potential energy surface

The close agreement in both energetics and geometric properties in the two sets of results validates the accuracy of the EANN PES. Consistent with previous theoretical studies<sup>20,65–67</sup> , an isolated CO molecule is found to adsorb perpendicularly on a face-centered cubic (fcc) site with C-terminal facing down. The adsorption energy of 1.42 eV is in good agreement with the experimental value of 1.4 eV<sup>68</sup> . When the CO molecule and O atom co-adsorb on the Pt(111) surface, the desorption energy of CO becomes 1.30 eV, which may be caused by the repulsion of the adsorbed O atom. The O atom also prefers an fcc site. The adsorbed CO can easily diffuse from the fcc site to the bridge site by overcoming a small potential barrier of ~0.05 eV. In contrast, the diffusion of the O atom on the Pt(111) surface is more difficult due to its relatively high diffusion barrier (0.57 eV).

### Free energy surface 

For free-energy calculations, the choice of suitable collective variables (CVs) is of paramount importance for all CV-based enhanced sampling methods, including WT-MetaD<sup>58</sup> . During CO oxidation on Pt(111), for example, adsorbed CO can be competitively consumed through desorption with a comparable barrier to the reaction. Consequently, these two elementary steps are not separable in space and time<sup>7</sup> . As discussed in detail in SI, the CO desorption channel cannot be excluded when sampling the phase space for CO oxidation in the WT-MetaD simulations. In addition, the exchange of the two oxygen atoms results in twoidenticalCO oxidation processes. To describe thismulti-channel surface process, as illustrated in Fig. S3, it is essential to introduce three CVs, including the permutationally equivalent distances between the C atom and each O atom (r1 and r2) and the height of the C atom above the top layer of the surface (h). As shown in Fig. S4, the PMFs for CO oxidation calculated using an insufficient number of CVs deviate from the result obtained with three CVs. This indicates that inadequate CV selection fails to sample the relevant phase space properly, leading to inaccuracies in the free-energy calculations. Notably, by including h as one of the CVs, the CO desorption pathway is naturally incorporated into the enhanced sampling. This allows us to obtain the PMF and rate coefficients for this channel without additional computational effort. 

Based on the constructed EANN PES, the three-dimensional FES, F(r1, r2, h), was computed using WT-MetaD from 500 to 700 K at a 50 K interval. For visualization, we integrate over r1 to obtain the twodimensional FES at 600 K, shown in Fig. 2. As can be seen, the initial 

state (CO* + O*) accesses the reaction channel through TS1 largely along the r2 coordinate, leading to the production of gaseous CO2. The free-energy barrier (TS1) is 1.02 eV. On the other hand, the CO desorption channel also has a free-energy barrier TS3 (1.17 eV). Interestingly, the existence of a small exit channel barrier for CO desorption is consistent with the earlier work of Hu and coworkers<sup>24</sup> . 

### Recrossing dynamics 

Figure 3 compares the time-dependent transmission coefficients, κ, computed by Eq. (4) in the RFT detailed in the Methods section, for both CO oxidation and CO desorption at 500, 600, and 700 K. For the oxidation reaction (Fig. 3a), the transmission coefficients were calculated by placing the dividing surface at TS1 (r2 = 1.98 Å). κ decreases rapidly to a plateau at ~ 0.82, indicating a minor recrossing effect at TS1. As shown by an exemplary trajectory in SI (Fig. S6a), this recrossing can be largely attributed to the inefficient energy transfer from the C-O stretching vibration, which is aligned with the RC at TS1, to the Z-component of the CO2 center of mass translation, as required for the molecule to escape. 

For the desorption process (Fig. 3b), the transmission coefficients were computed by placing the dividing surface at TS3 (h = 3.04 Å). During sampling in the constrained ensemble, the CO₂ region was not accessed because of the existence of a very high energy barrier separating TS3 from CO2 configurations, as clearly seen from Fig. 2. The recrossing effect of CO desorption on Pt(111) is much more pronounced than NO desorption on Pd(111)<sup>51</sup> , with κ converging to ~ 0.3 within less than 1 ps. This significant recrossing is likely due to the rebound of CO from the surface, which causes it to recross the barrier, as illustrated by an exemplary recrossing trajectory in SI (Fig. S7a). 

Interestingly, the CO oxidation and desorption channels are dominated by two different types of recrossing behaviors. In the CO oxidation channel, the majority of recrossing trajectories start with positive velocities towards the product side (CO2) and recross TS1 to arrive at the reactant side (CO* + O*). In contrast, for CO desorption, most recrossing trajectories correspond to those with initial negative velocities towards the CO adsorption state, which then recross TS3 after the rebound from the surface to yield desorbed CO products. This peculiar kind of recrossing process has also been discussed in the development of variational TST and in proton transfer processes in solution<sup>45,69</sup> . 

Both of the above behaviors lead to different extents of recrossing, resulting in different transmission coefficients. It is worth noting that these mechanisms are illustrated for better understanding of the recrossing process, and their relative proportions may vary with the place of the dividing surface. To assess the appropriateness of the chosen dividing surface, committor analysis was performed for different positions of the dividing surface. A more balanced commitment probability distribution indicates a better choice of the dividing surface. The committor analysis presented in Fig. S8 indicates that the chosen dividing surface for CO oxidation is reasonable, although that for CO desorption is less optimal. However, as introduced in the Methods section, the final RFT rate coefficient is independent of the specific choice of the dividing surface.

### Well-tempered metadynamics 

For gas phase elementary reactions, it is often sufficient to define a single RC<sup>47,48</sup> . For complex reactions such as those occurring on surfaces, however, multiple elementary processes might be coupled<sup>27</sup> . To deal with such problems, one often needs to introduce multiple collective variables to map out the multi-dimensional PES<sup>75,76</sup> , which can be then projected along a specific RC to obtain the free-energy profile, known as the PMF for the reaction. For this purpose, an efficient enhanced sampling method is metadynamics<sup>77</sup> , in which Gaussian shaped potentials are added in the CVs to flatten the FES. ### Computational details 

All MD simulations for free energy calculations were performed using the LAMMPS package<sup>79</sup> supplemented by PLUMED v2.4 plugin<sup>80</sup> . The canonical ensemble from 500 to 700 K was sampled for 40 ns using a Nosé-Hoover thermostat and a time step of 0.2 fs. Regarding WTMetaD, the initial Gaussian height (W0) was set to 0.02 eV, the widths (σi) of the Gaussians added along each CV were all taken to be 0.2 Å, and the time interval (τ) for adding Gaussians was 80 fs. The bias factor (T + ΔT)/T was chosen based on the maximum potential barrier for the elementary reactions<sup>58</sup> . Finally, kTSTðTÞ was obtained according to Eq. (6), following a one-dimensional (1D) PMF along the chosen RC for CO2 formation (r1 or r2) or CO desorption (h) channel, after the multidimensional FES was integrated over other CVs. κðt ! 1Þ was computed by sampling an ensemble of trajectories (say 100,000) at the barrier of the corresponding 1D PMF and allowing them to evolve towards either side. The detailed procedure of the κðt ! 1Þ calculations was provided in SI. These quantities were obtained using a modified version of the RPMDrate program<sup>47</sup> . The computed freeenergy profile was carefully checked for convergence with regard to the sampling time. 

For comparison, we also computed the rate coefficients of CO oxidation and desorption with the Eyring’s TST based on two translational entropy models of the adsorbates, namely the HA and the HT models<sup>18,19</sup> . A brief introduction to the HT model is provided in the SI. It should be noted that the TS for the CO oxidation reaction has a degeneracy of 3, owing to the three equivalent approaches of CO* toward the O* atom occupying the fcc site. 

Experimentally, the oxygen coverage was 0.04 ML and the dose of a single CO pulse was merely 2 × 10<sup>-5</sup> monolayers<sup>7</sup> , which reasonably fulfilled the low-coverage limit. The CO doses are so small in experiment that the O* coverage is independent of time during the reaction<sup>5,7</sup> , for which the CO oxidation reaction can be treated as a pseudo 1<sup>st</sup> -order reaction. To facilitate comparison, the CO oxidation rate coefficients computed by the second-order RFT expression in units of cm<sup>2</sup> /s are converted to pseudo first-order results with units of s<sup>−1</sup> by including the unit of O* coverage, as done the experimental work<sup>7</sup> .
