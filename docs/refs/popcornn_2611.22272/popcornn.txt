                                                                                       Abstract
                                                    Transition states govern chemical reaction rates and outcomes, yet locating them
                                                    remains a central challenge in computational chemistry. Double-ended search
                                                    methods find transition states given reactant and product structures, but gen-
                                                    erally rely on discretized path representations developed for density functional
                                                    theory, where potential energy surface evaluations are expensive. Machine learned




                                                                                           1
     interatomic potentials (MLIPs) dramatically reduce this cost and leverage auto-
     matic differentiation to access higher-order derivatives efficiently. We introduce
     Popcornn, a framework to find transition states by optimizing continuous neural
     network paths atop an MLIP with a parallelized adaptive numerical integrator.
     This combination eliminates path discretization artifacts, reduces hyperparam-
     eter sensitivity, and enables non-local optimization to overcome local features
     using second-order potential-energy-surface information. Popcornn consistently
     recovers more correct transition states than standard methods such as nudged
     elastic band and growing string, across MLIPs on thousands of organic reac-
     tions, establishing it as a robust, automation-ready method for high-throughput
     transition state search. Popcornn’s infrastructure is flexible in path objective,
     integration strategy, and optimization algorithm, making it a practical tool and
     platform for investigating open questions in MLIP-based path optimization.

     Keywords: Transition states, path optimization, machine learning interatomic
     potentials, reaction networks




1 Introduction
The quantum mechanical potential energy surface (PES) describes how an atomistic
system’s energy changes during conformational rearrangement and chemical transfor-
mations. The most chemically pertinent areas of the PES are stationary points: minima
define stable molecules, and first-order saddle points define transition states (TSs) that
describe transformations of reactant molecules into product species. Accurately and
efficiently locating the TSs on the PES is critical for understanding complex reactive
combustion [1, 2], electrolyte decomposition [3–7], heterogeneous [8] and homogeneous
catalysis [9], polymer transformations [10], and more.
     PES minima can be reliably found with a variety of different local optimiza-
tion algorithms using only iterative energy and gradient (1st order and quasi-second
order) methods, starting from nearly any reasonable initial guess structure [11, 12].
In contrast, single-ended TS optimization depends heavily on the quality of the initial
guess (i.e., how close the structure is to the desired TS), the optimization algorithm
employed, and often the need to calculate Hessian (2nd order) information. TS opti-
mization for chemical transformations is typically carried out with density functional
theory (DFT). However, when Hessian (2nd order) information is used, DFT even-
tually becomes intractable for large systems due to quartic scaling with the number
of basis functions. As automatically-differentiable machine learned interatomic poten-
tials (MLIPs) progress towards being reliable DFT surrogates, improved accessibility
of second-order information has enabled more robust single-ended TS optimization
[13–16].
     Even with readily-available Hessians, single-ended TS optimization remains fun-
damentally constrained by the need for a reasonably good initial guess TS structure
[13, 16]. Experts can build such structures by hand, structural templates can be
employed for specific reaction classes [17], and generative models can now propose



                                             2
TS structures from reactants and products [18–21] or from reactants alone [22]. How-
ever, these generative models are limited in chemical scope by their TS training data;
currently, standard datasets consist of main-group reactions [22, 23]. Therefore, auto-
mated initial guess construction across the chemical diversity of complex reaction
networks [24, 25] remains an open challenge.
    An alternative and more automation-friendly approach is double-ended TS
search [12], which locates the TS given reactant and product structures by reformu-
lating the task as a path optimization. It is the natural tool for reaction networks
built by enumerating candidate reactant–product pairs [5, 24, 26]. The path can be
thought of as an optimizable function xθ (t) which maps a point on a reaction coor-
dinate or “reaction time” t to a 3D structure. The path is constrained to return the
reactant structure when t = 0 and the product structure when t = 1: xθ (0) = xR and
xθ (1) = xP . Thus, the transformation of the reactant to the product must necessarily
be accomplished along the path. The optimization objective for most double-ended
path optimization schemes is derived from or can be interpreted as a path integral:

where ℓ(x(t)) quantifies the local “path quality” at reaction time t. In the simplest
case, ℓ(x(t)) can be the potential energy E (x(t)), and the minimization objective LE
is the total energy along the path. Decades of work, including the nudged elastic band
(NEB) [27, 28] and growing string method (GSM) [29–32], have been dedicated to
obtaining the minimum energy path (MEP) by minimizing LE . The highest energy
point along the MEP is guaranteed to be a TS, and the MEP is the gradient descent
path from the TS to the reactant and product minima.
    Notably, NEB and GSM were developed under the constraints of performing path
optimization atop a DFT PES. Despite avoiding prohibitively expensive Hessians, cal-

culating a structure’s total energy and atomic forces with DFT still scales as O(Ne−     ),
becoming computationally challenging or intractable for larger systems. Traditionally,
to make the path optimization computationally practical, the path (and its integral
Eq. 1) is discretized to a set of discrete structures (images) such that only tens of DFT
calculations are needed at each optimization iteration. However, path discretization
has a variety of negative consequences: the potential to miss PES features between
discrete images; complexities around how to place images along the path; maintaining
path connectivity with fictitious spring forces; the need to treat a “climbing image”
differently from the rest of the path; and the introduction of many hyperparameters
(related to these details) that often need to be carefully selected by the user for each
reaction. All of these consequences are anathema to automation. Perhaps most prob-
lematic, path optimization proceeds locally such that each image’s position is updated
pursuant only to the energy and forces at that image and its two neighboring images,
preventing the path from cohesively mutating into an entirely different track along
the PES.
    Independent of the path representation, the MEP optimization objective is criti-
cally hindered by the MEP’s sensitivity to local structures that do not affect the TS.
Although LE only requires PES first derivatives at each optimization iteration and the


                                              3
MEP has attractive mathematical properties [33, 34], it is also sensitive to every part
of the path. This sensitivity is a crucial drawback, making the optimization landscape
substantially more complex and difficult to navigate. In practice, the primary goal
of path optimization should be to locate the TS. With the TS in hand, we can then
obtain the MEP and/or confirm the associated endpoints via intrinsic reaction coor-
dinate (IRC) or quasi-IRC [33, 35] calculations. However, directly targeting the TS –
rather than the MEP – requires a force-based path objective [36]; but a force-based
path objective necessitates the calculation of second derivatives along the path at each
iteration. While intractable with DFT, this presents an opportunity for improved path
optimization methodology atop differentiable MLIPs.
    In this work, we present a software platform for MLIP-based double-ended TS
search that we call Popcornn : “Path Optimization with a Continuous Representation
Neural Network.” Popcornn leverages a neural network to parameterize the continuous
path from reactant to product, combined with a GPU-parallelized adaptive numerical
path integrator to calculate the path objective – a critical combination we find neces-
sary to ensure comprehensive PES sampling and to avoid many issues of traditional
path discretization. This combination further facilitates global and cohesive path opti-
mization by differentiating the path objective through the MLIP with respect to the
path’s weights and biases. By default, Popcornn employs a force-based optimization
objective that directly targets the TS rather than seeking the MEP. We demonstrate
that, with fixed default settings, Popcornn consistently achieves improved robustness
and recovery of intended TSs, when compared with NEB and GSM, across multiple
MLIPs and thousands of in-distribution and out-of-distribution test reactions. Addi-
tionally, Popcornn is engineered to be flexible with respect to path objective, numerical
integration strategy, optimization algorithm, and path initialization strategy. Thus, it
is both of immediate practical utility for double-ended TS search atop a pre-trained
MLIP, and it is also envisioned as a powerful platform for subsequent investigation
and further improvement of MLIP-based path optimization methodologies.

Critically, we note that changing any internal neuron parameter in xθ (t) modifies all
atomic positions across the entire reaction path, enabling non-local path mutation
rather than local bead perturbations.
    To realize our continuous formulation’s (Eqs. 1 and 2) full capabilities, the loss
must also be evaluated via adaptive numerical integration, rather than a coarse or
other rigid summation scheme. Thus, unlike conventional methods that approximate
the loss (Eq. 1) using discrete points, we evaluate the path integral with the GPU-
parallelized adaptive numerical path integrator PadaQuad [40] (Fig. 1b). PadaQuad
globally updates the entire path by integrating the gradient of the loss with respect to
all network parameters along the entire path (Eq. 5), driving cohesive path optimiza-
tion via gradient descent (Fig. 1c). The adaptivity of PadaQuad is central to avoiding
fixed discretization, and it focuses PES evaluations in regions of high variation and
large contributions to the integral while limiting evaluations in slowly varying regions
(Supplementary Note E). The adaptive numerical integration of Eq. 1 precludes the
need for fictitious spring force terms. Instead, PadaQuad does this by adaptively
adding, removing, and adjusting evaluation points based on the integral error, indepen-
dent of the PES value. This adaptivity places evaluations where needed, removing the
need for spring forces to affix them and creating an efficient and error-bound evaluation
of the path. We provide more details on PadaQuad in Supplementary Note E.

Numerically, this gradient can be calculated in two ways: taking the gradient of the
completed integral; or integrating the gradient of the integrand. In the former case,
the evaluation points are determined by the loss integral since the gradient is taken
after. In the latter case, the gradient is more accurate because the evaluation points
are chosen to minimize the error of the gradient by placing them where the gradi-
ent of the loss varies the most. We use the latter formulation, though Popcornn and
PadaQuad support both options. The gradient is one order higher than the highest
order derivative in the quantity ℓ. That is, if the integrand ℓ contains only the poten-
tial energy (like in NEB), only the first derivative of the PES (atomic forces) would be
needed. On the other hand, if ℓ involves the forces (VRE and pVRE), then the second
derivative (the Hessian) is necessary for the path differentiation. The steep computa-
tional cost of DFT-based second derivatives has prevented force-based path objectives
from being realistically viable, until the recent advent of differentiable MLIPs that are
sufficiently accurate to describe reactive PESs for chemical systems of interest [13].
    The LpVRE loss admits a natural force-based convergence criterion based on the
optimization gradient (Eq. 5). Established energy-minimization methods like NEB
and GSM leverage modified forces (perpendicular gradients on the images and spring
forces) to determine the convergence of the optimization [28]. The LpVRE loss iso-
lates and targets the TS (Supplementary Note B.2), while removing intermediate
extrema along xθ (t). Specifically, the gradient of LpVRE becomes proportional to the
TS force as the path converges (Eq. B39). Therefore, the gradient of LpVRE is a
physically consistent counterpart to convergence criteria used by the aforementioned

    To understand the differences between paths optimized with the LpVRE and LVRE
losses, we demonstrate Popcornn with both loss functions on the model Müller-Brown
potential, revealing the advantages of LpVRE . Figure 2 shows the Popcornn optimiza-
tion of xθ (t) on the Müller-Brown potential for the LVRE and LpVRE losses. We observe
xθ (t) optimized with LpVRE converges to the TS roughly three times faster than with
LVRE . The LpVRE optimization quickly locates the TS and converges to a stable path,
while the LVRE optimization struggles to navigate the PES within the first 200 steps
due to LVRE ’s more complex locally-aware loss landscape. Given that our stated goal
is to locate the TS as robustly as possible, the TS-focused LpVRE is the superior path
objective. For the remainder of this work, we use LpVRE as our optimization objec-
tive unless otherwise stated. We provide more detailed discussion and intuition of the
LVRE and LpVRE losses in Supplementary Note B.
    It is well established that the quality of the initial path provided for path opti-
mization dramatically impacts the success rate of the path optimization task and the
likelihood of recovering the intended TS [44]. In the molecular context, the geodesic
method of Zhu et al. [45] and the IDPP method [46] are commonly used. Both leverage


                                                7
discretized path optimizations with custom objectives atop bespoke analytic pair-
wise potentials to improve upon a naive linear path. This avoids steric clashes that
could yield critically problematic DFT convergence or erroneous MLIP predictions.
Popcornn extends its continuous path representation, adaptive numerical integration,
and backpropagation-optimization machinery to initial path construction. Similar to
MLIPs, one can add new path initializations to the Popcornn ecosystem [47]. We imple-
mented a continuous path geodesic optimization atop the pairwise potential given by
Zhu et al. [45], and we refer to this approach as Popcornn -geodesic. As we show below,
this substantially improves path optimization outcomes.

2.2 Chemical reaction evaluations
To evaluate Popcornn ’s ability to robustly locate intended transition states in a
systematic manner, we conduct large-scale experiments across different MLIPs on
both in-distribution and out-of-distribution reactions. By leveraging reaction test sets
containing thousands of examples, we seek to ensure that we are drawing reliable,
statistically significant conclusions, while demonstrating Popcornn ’s robustness and
high-throughput capabilities. We find the reaction path in three steps: we first opti-
mize the endpoint structures on the MLIP (Fig. 3a); secondly, we construct an initial
path (between the optimized endpoints) atop a pairwise potential using discretized
geodesic or Popcornn -geodesic (Fig. 3b); finally, we optimize the path atop the MLIP
(Fig. 3c). To find the TS, we apply a TS optimization method to the structure along
the optimized path with maximal energy and minimal forces. To validate the pro-
posed TSs, we apply (quasi)IRC calculations to recover endpoints (Fig. 3d) that are
then compared with initial endpoints (Fig. 3e). Experimental success, as measured
by graph matching between initial endpoints and IRC endpoints, is categorized by a
quality hierarchy:
• Intended: Both IRC endpoints match both initial endpoints.
• Partially intended: One IRC endpoint matches one initial endpoint.
• Unintended: Neither IRC endpoint matches either initial endpoint; however, the
  IRC endpoints also do not match each other, i.e., a reaction has still occurred.
• No reaction: IRC endpoints match, i.e., the transition state found corresponds only
  to a conformational change, not a reaction.
    We benchmark Popcornn against the two most common path optimzation meth-
ods, NEB [27, 28] and GSM [29–32]. For NEB, we compare two different initial path
construction strategies: discretized geodesic and Popcornn -geodesic. For GSM, initial
path construction is not required; the path is built concurrently with optimization.
For Popcornn, Popcornn -geodesic constructs the initial path prior to Popcornn opti-
mization atop the MLIP with the pVRE optimization objective. We note that for
each method we run all experiments with multiple different sets of hyperparameters
and present results from only the single best-performing set. This procedure is most
representative of performance in a simple high-throughput context, yielding a fair
comparison between methods. Further discussion of NEB hyperparameters is provided
in Section 4.4. Supplementary Note F presents ablation tests across bead density and
path initialization methods for NEB.

3 Discussion
Popcornn is a robust double-ended TS search framework explicitly designed to lever-
age the distinct advantages of MLIPs. MLIP efficiency and differentiability via simple
backpropagation allow us to address fundamental weaknesses in previous approaches
by employing a continuous path representation coupled with adaptive numerical inte-
gration of a path objective that directly targets the TS rather than the entire MEP.
Further, the batched parallelization of our adaptive integrator allows Popcornn to
take full advantage of modern GPU hardware. In contrast to typical small-scale com-
parisons of path optimization methods, we demonstrate Popcornn ’s advantages via
high-throughput evaluations with thousands of reactions at various levels of difficulty.
While we evaluate robustness and computational runtime, we consider robustness to
be the dominant factor that should govern method selection in the MLIP context. This
is because probing the PES will become extremely fast/cheap with future ML hard-
ware and software advancements. Popcornn consistently outperforms NEB and GSM
across multiple MLIPs and reaction sets while simultaneously reducing the number of
hyperparameters that a user must choose, making it well-suited for high-throughput
double-ended TS search.
    We attribute much of Popcornn ’s success to the symbiotic combination of a contin-
uous path representation xθ (t) and an adaptive numerical integrator. The importance
of incorporating careful numerical considerations within scientific machine learning
methods has been highlighted previously [54–59]. Although properly integrating Eq. 1
requires a continuous path, the continuous path alone is not sufficient. Earlier work by
Ramakrishnan et al. [38] employ neural network paths with fixed and discrete integral
approximation schemes that are still vulnerable to missing essential PES structure
along the path. Popcornn, instead, leverages the continuous nature of the path to
enable adaptive numerical integration. We therefore stress the importance of simulta-
neously having a continuous path xθ (t) and an accurate numerical integration scheme
built into the path optimization.
    Popcornn is a highly flexible framework built to adapt to and grow with advancing
machine learning, chemistry, and optimization techniques. Specifically, by modular-
izing the path representation, PES, path integrator, and path objective, a user can
swap any functionality for their own method. Various MLIPs, including Newton-
Net [13, 49], MACE [52, 53], ANI-1x [60, 61], LeftNet [51], Orb [62], and UMA [63], are
already supported, and new models can be easily implemented. While we used vanilla



                                          12
neural networks for the path representation in this work, spline representations are
also available, and more sophisticated architectures are possible. Similarly, the objec-
tive integrator and the objective function can be replaced. The modular architecture
empowers chemists to systematically investigate how path representation, objective,
optimization, integration, and PES calculations interact; an open methodological ques-
tion that has thus far been difficult to address with monolithic implementations. We
envision the Popcornn framework as a powerful software framework for subsequent
path optimization methodology development as well as for establishing additional
diverse reaction evaluations.
    While the Popcornn framework and the test systems used here are all molecular
and non-periodic, we are currently generalizing Popcornn to periodic systems, allow-
ing it to be employed for double-ended transition state optimization for catalysis and
material systems. For such periodic non-molecular systems, future work will deter-
mine if the geodesic loss and the pairwise potential from Zhu et al. [45] (designed for
small molecules) will be sufficient, or if a new interpolation loss (e.g., a continuous
implementation of IDPP) will be needed.
    Reaction networks are broadly constructed in one of two ways [25]. Exploration-
based construction grows the network outward from the reactants with a single-ended
TS search, discovering rather than presupposing products [22, 64]. To avoid combina-
torial blowup in the number of reactions, one must constrain which transformations are
sampled and which species are expanded as the network deepens. Enumeration-based
construction instead bounds the chemical space up front by enumerating candidate
species and reactant–product pairs [5, 24, 26]. Each pair then requires a double-ended
TS search, which imposes no restriction on the bond-change pattern connecting the
endpoints. Popcornn is designed for this second setting. It is also complementary to
generative TS models [18–22], which can rapidly propose accurate TSs within the
chemistry represented in their training data but require curated sets of converged TSs
to train. Since Popcornn is a tool that consumes MLIPs, its scope is only set by the
underlying pre-trained MLIP, rather than any one particular dataset coverage. For
example, Popcornn already works with foundation MLIPs trained on OMol25 [63, 65]
already span transition metals and diverse charge and spin states, chemistry for which
reactive TS data remain scarce.
    Finally, as MLIPs continue to improve in speed, accuracy, and generality [66], we
believe that robust transition state search via Popcornn and subsequent improvements
atop our software framework will enable the construction of massive chemical reaction
networks, allowing us to understand and eventually design complex reactive processes
in electrolytes, catalysis, polymers, and beyond.

4 Methods
4.1 Interfacing with Popcornn
Here, we describe Popcornn in greater detail, walk through important design choices,
and outline key steps in applying Popcornn . This section generally follows the Pop-
cornn workflow and highlights key components that a user may wish to adapt to



                                          13
their specific problem. Supplementary Note G gives pseudocode for each step, and
Algorithm 1 summarizes a complete run.

4.1.1 Input Endpoints into Popcornn
The Popcornn package takes optimized endpoints as inputs. Popcornn itself does not
optimize the endpoints and expects the user to have done so with their preferred
method. In this work, the endpoints are first relaxed with BFGS on the MLIP PES.
Popcornn fixes the input endpoints in Cartesian coordinates, thus requiring the end-
points to be translationally and rotationally aligned. See Supplementary Note A for
more discussion.

4.1.2 The Path Model
A single 2-layer perceptron is used for the path representation, following Eq. 2. Con-
cretely, the neural network fθ is Linear(1, 128) → GELU () → Linear(128, 3N ). The
GELU activation is chosen because of its smoothness [67]. Supplementary Note A
provides more details on the path representation, and Algorithm 2 (Supplementary
Note G.2) shows how positions, velocities, energies and forces are evaluated along the
path.

4.1.4 Interfacing with MLIPs
We have interfaced Popcornn with various MLIPs, including NewtonNet [13, 49],

4.1.5 TS Determination
When searching for the TS along xθ (t), we found that both minimizing the PES gra-
dient and maximizing energy led to the most robust method. The TS is the highest
energy point along xθ (t), but simply selecting the highest energy point among the
evaluated xθ (t) points may not be sufficiently precise if the adaptive integration eval-
uations are too sparse. Instead, we find points between the evaluated path points at
which dE/dt = 0 (calculated using Eq. B29) since this is more stable and has higher
precision than a finite difference comparison between energy calculations. First, we
determine where dE/dt changes sign within the cached values from the latest Pop-
cornn optimization evaluation. This can result in multiple TS candidates. Second, we
find the TS time of each pair by linearly interpolating between the cached time points
and solve for t that satisfies dE (t)/dt = 0. Finally, we evaluate the PES energy of all
the TS time candidates and determine t∗ as the highest energy point; this discards
sign changes at energy minima. Algorithm 5 (Supplementary Note G.5) gives the full
procedure.

4.2 Popcornn Path Optimization
4.2.1 Parallel Evaluations and Memory Management
The loss integration is handled by PadaQuad [40]: a parallelized adaptive quadra-
ture numerical integrator for path integrals with backpropagation capabilities. The
adaptive numerical integration is necessary to properly evaluate Eq. 1. PadaQuad
leverages the knowledge of the path to parallelize the integration, improving speed by
orders of magnitude. PadaQuad also uses an adaptive memory management scheme
to fully utilize GPU parallelism. We apply the absolute and relative tolerances of 0
and 1, respectively, for the numerical integration. Hegazy et al. [40] and Supplemen-
tary Note E provide more detailed discussions. Algorithms 3 and 4 (Supplementary
Notes G.3 and G.4) give the gradient evaluation and the adaptive quadrature.

4.2.2 Optimization Hyperparameters
We use the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 10−3 for both initial path construc-
tion atop the initialization potential and final optimization atop the MLIP potential.
Each of these optimizations used its own optimizer; that is, the optimizer was not
shared between them. We run each optimization for up to 1000 steps, or stop as it
reaches the gradient convergence of 10−2 for initial path construction and 10−1 for
path optimization (corresponding to TS forces of 0.05 eV/Å). Supplementary Note C


                                           15
provides more detailed discussions, and Algorithm 1 shows where each of these settings
enters.

4.3 TS Optimization
The TS acquired from Popcornn or other reference methods are optimized to a first-
order saddle point on the MLIP using Sella [68] with the full Hessian at every step
[13] with a convergence threshold of 0.01 eV/Å for up to 1000 steps. For retrieved
TSs, forward and backwards IRC calculations are run using SellaIRC [68] with a
convergence threshold of 0.01 eV/Å for up to another 1000 steps. For organic reactions,
the endpoints are compared using connectivity graphs created by Open Babel [69].

4.4 Reference Methods
For a fair comparison, we provide the same inputs to each method and apply the same
TS optimization to all outputs. Specifically, we give each method the same optimized
endpoints. The output TSs of each method are processed in exactly the same way.
    We use “improved tangent” NEB [27] with 10, 20, and 100 beads (k=5.0) and
GSM [29] with 9 and 25 beads and a convergence threshold of 0.05 eV/Å. NEBs are
optimized with the MDMin optimizer (dt=0.02, maxstep=0.1) in ASE for 2000 steps
in total, where the first 1000 steps are without the climbing image and the last 1000
steps are with the climbing image, with a convergence threshold of 0.05 eV/Å [28].
All other hyperparameters are in ASE 3.28.0 and pyGSM defaults.

A.4     Internal Versus Cartesian Coordinates
Our path operates in Cartesian coordinates instead of the internal coordinates.
Although empirical evidence has shown that internal coordinates are important for
geometry optimization [68], transforming from internal coordinates to Cartesian is
non-unique and generally difficult. This difficulty is in part because internal coordi-
nates can have singularities, and optimal coordinates generally change throughout the
reaction.
   One of the primary benefits of internal coordinates is the grouped movement of
atoms by one or a few coordinates. This feature can be captured by the neural network
path, which can learn the optimal time-dependent internal representation. Thus, the



                                               29
neural network path effectively learns the time-dependent internal coordinates and
their transformation.
   The input molecules must be properly positioned before being passed to Popcornn.
This includes handling the translational and rotational degrees of freedom. This is a
non-trivial task that is best handled by specialty external packages [64, 72].

A.5     Complexity of the Path Model
The neural network fθ (t) is much smaller than the MLIP, serving only as a flexible
representation of the path, not as a sophisticated regressor. Here, we use a simple fully-
connected neural network instead of any invariant or equivariant architecture. Such
inductive biases are commonplace in many MLIPs [39, 52]. We found that many of
these inductive biases can be detrimental to our path optimization. Moreover, allowing
the neural network to learn optimal coordinate systems and transformations removes
the need for such inductive biases.
   The locality assumption is a very common inductive bias found in most all MLIPs.
Here, the representation of each atom is updated by information of atoms within
some radius. This, however, can be detrimental for Popcornn, which can optimize
the reaction between two separated molecules. Imagine the simplest possible reaction
H+H          H2 where the reactant atoms are separated much further than a typical
radius used in the locality assumption. This path, therefore, is generally not local.
Although some of the non-locality is captured by the linear prior, it is likely that this
inductive bias would hamper the optimization.
   Another common and important inductive bias is rotation and translation invari-
ance. However, the linear prior breaks the individual translational and rotational
symmetries of endpoints. Given the importance of the linear prior in this work, we
consider that incorporating invariant or equivariant architectures for the perturbation
neural network will lead to complications.

Supplementary Note B                              Popcornn Optimization
                                                  Losses
For double-ended TS searches, we use the assumption of simple TS theory that the
path is simply a tool for finding the TS, and other points along the entire path (e.g.,
MEP, the gradient descent path from the TS, etc.) are not needed. Hence Popcornn
is designed to work with all losses that can be written as a path integral (Eq. 1). In
this work, we investigate three path integral losses: LVRE in Eq. 3; LpVRE in Eq. 4;
and Lgeo in Eq. 7. We have demonstrated their behaviors in Fig. 2, and we provide
further discussion on their physical interpretation below.

B.1    The Variational Reaction Energy (VRE) Loss
The LVRE loss is only guaranteed to find the gradient descent path from any stationary
point to which it converges. This is because the LVRE path can converge onto a
high-order saddle point or even a maximum, instead of the TS. This issue is more
pronounced on a high-dimensional PES with many stationary points of various orders.
To demonstrate this failure mode on a low-dimensional system, we show that the LVRE
path converges to the PES maximum of the Wolfe potential, in Fig. B4.

LpVRE . This provides a complementary view of why pVRE focuses optimization on
transition-state structure: flat or monotonic portions of the path do not contribute
to the first-order signal, whereas energy extrema contribute through delta-function
weights.
    This behavior is further intuited by the fact that xθ (t) accumulates no loss when
traveling on an isoenergy line. For xθ (t) to traverse an isoenergy line, its velocity must
be perpendicular to the force, and by Eq. 4 the integrand is 0. The ability for xθ (t)
to freely travel along isoenergy paths aligns with the statistical nature of chemical
reactions, where structures of the same energy are equally likely to occur and thus
xθ (t) should be able to freely travel between them.

B.3     A Combined Variational Loss: Variational Error
The work of Birkholz and Schlegel [36] defines a non-negative variational error loss to
find the steepest descent reaction path. The variational error

                                 εVRE = LVRE − LpVRE                                (B40)

is the difference between the VRE and pVRE losses. Minimizing this loss extremizes
the dot product of LpVRE : aligning the gradient of the PES with the velocity. However,
all gradient descent paths minimize εVRE ; in fact, Eq. B40 is independent of the
stationary point. That is, one can reach εVRE = 0 by going over any feature where
the PES gradient is 0: any PES peak, any saddle point, any PES minima. Therefore,
once xθ (t) corresponds to one of these extrema, there is likely no way to optimize
xθ (t) towards the correct TS. Instead, the path will be optimized to align xθ (t) with
the PES gradient while staying at this extreme.

B.4     Losses for Path Initialization: Geodesic Loss
Properly initializing the reaction path is a crucial step (Fig. 3b), and doing so incor-
rectly can place xθ (t) in a PES region where it will be optimized to the incorrect TS.
That is, the path initialization effectively confines the optimization to a PES region
and must therefore be sensitive to the region with the correct TS. This is necessary
for multiple reasons: often accurate optimizations can be more expensive and cannot
optimize over large PES regions; MLIPs can become undefined in some PES regions;
and it is generally prohibitively challenging or expensive to optimize xθ (t) across many
large PES structures to a new PES region on complex PES surfaces using especially
ab initio methods. Therefore, a quality path initializer must leverage chemical biases
to select the PES region, balance PES evaluation cost, and often smooth the PES to
allow easier optimization. Hence, one often uses a pairwise potential (Eq. 6) that is
fast to evaluate, smooths the PES in a principled way, and still maintains chemical
expectations.


                                            35
    Optimizing the initial path over the pairwise PES with LpVRE and LVRE suffers
from catastrophic failures caused by approximations in the pairwise PES. For LpVRE
this stems from the fact that on the pairwise PES the reactants and products are
generally not at the minima, and there is often no TS in between. Since Eq. 4 has a
monotonic integrand, the integral would be a constant E (xθ (1)) − E (xθ (0)) regardless
of the path. Thus LpVRE is insensitive to the initial path in the common scenarios
where reactant, product, and TS stationary points are missing and therefore cannot
optimize the path. In contrast, the LVRE path is sensitive to the entire path, instead of
a few special points like LpVRE . However, the total energy is not very indicative since
the lengthening of one bond will be canceled by the shortening of another bond for
a pairwise potential like Eq. 6. This makes LVRE insensitive to initial path changes.
Additionally, the energy can be reduced by compensating one bond motion with the
opposite motion from another bond; this increases the possibility of visiting an unlikely
structure where DFT and MLIPs may become undefined.
    The geodesic loss (Lgeo ) [45] can be intuited as minimizing the amount of dis-
placement atoms must make while being rearranged from the reactant to the product
structure. This imposes a chemical intuition that the optimal reaction path can gen-
erally be optimized from the “least-motion” or the spatially shortest path. Instead
of summing the pairwise energy contributions before evaluating the force or the loss
integrand, Lgeo squares these individual pairwise contributions to minimize them
separately

them. While the TS losses speed up the reaction path convergence [36], they lead to
inferior paths due to faster convergence. Many of these paths corresponded to multi-
step reactions. Given our goal is to find single-step reaction TS, the TS losses were not
well suited for our specific task. We do believe that the TS losses can be well-suited for
other tasks, where one is interested in multi-step reactions or in higher TS structure
precision. We also note that the TS losses can be turned on at or near the end of the
Popcornn optimization (Supplementary Note D) to avoid path optimization effects
while still improving the TS itself.

Supplementary Note D                      Curriculum Optimization
                                          and Popcornn GSM
Popcornn can apply dynamic weighting to the path integral loss in a curriculum learn-
ing approach. Curriculum learning is a machine learning technique that dynamically
alters the loss to emphasize specific contributions the user wishes the optimization to
focus on [74, 75]. Heuristically, this is similar to the growing string method (GSM),
which optimizes end of strings that are continuously growing from the initial and
final points. Popcornn is equipped with a growing-string-like curriculum that initially
increases/decreases the path loss contributions near to and far from the endpoints.
The curriculum ends by evenly weighting all path points.
    Popcornn is also capable of increasing or decreasing specific loss contributions at
different points of the optimization. For example, the LTS can be absent in the first 3/4
of the path optimization (avoiding convergence to an inferior path) and then increased
at the end to improve the TS. Similarly, paths optimized with LpVRE converge faster
and find better TS than LVRE , but can ignore PES structures by traversing isoenergy
lines. This can make a LpVRE path difficult to interpret and will not resolve any local
minima. To leverage the superior LpVRE performance and create a more interpretable
path that resolves local minima (intermediate equilibrium structures), one can add
LVRE to the LpVRE loss near the end of the optimization.

Supplementary Note E                     Parallelized Adaptive
                                         Numerical Integration and
                                         PadaQuad
Adaptive numerical integration is necessary to leverage the continuous path xθ (t).
When xθ (t) is evaluated on a fixed grid of reaction times, the loss depends only on the
structures at the grid points, which act as discrete images. These structures are set by
both the shape of the path and its velocity profile, i.e., how quickly the path moves
through each region. Changing the velocity profile, “re-timing” the path, moves the
images along the path without changing its shape. By changing the velocity profile,
images “fall” toward regions of low loss during optimization. For LE , the images fall
down the PES toward low-energy structures such as xR and xP . NEB prevents this by
adding fictitious spring forces that keep the images evenly spaced [27, 28], effectively
fixing the velocity profile [38].


                                           40
    For LVRE and LpVRE , re-timing alone can localize the images at stationary points
of the PES, such as xR , xP , and the TS, where the force vanishes. Although the
exact LVRE and LpVRE integrals are independent of the velocity profile, their fixed-
grid sums are not because re-timing changes which structures sit at the grid points.
The optimizer can therefore lower the fixed-grid loss without improving the path by
placing the grid points at stationary points while the path crosses the high-loss regions
between them. In the limit, xθ (t) rests at xR and xP for nearly the entire reaction
time and crosses between them within a single grid interval. The images then coincide
at the endpoints, and the fixed-grid loss vanishes regardless of the barrier the path
crosses.
    Instead of fixing the velocity profile through a fictitious spring force, the adaptive
integrator adds evaluation points where the integration error is large and removes
them where it is small, until the error falls below a set tolerance. Where the path
moves quickly, the integrand generally varies rapidly; the integrator adds points in
these places, so re-timing no longer lowers the loss. For LVRE and LpVRE , adaptive
integration therefore removes the need for spring forces and keeps the loss within a
set tolerance of the exact integral, so the gradient depends on the shape of the path
rather than its velocity profile.

E.1    PadaQuad
Standard numerical integrators used for machine learning tasks are generally designed
for ODEs [76] and require sequential evaluations in time, which would make Popcornn
computationally intractable. Instead, we use PadaQuad [40]: an adaptive numerical
quadrature method designed to parallelize path integration on GPUs with backpropa-
gation compatibility. PadaQuad, as an adaptive numerical quadrature method, divides
the integral into steps that are evaluated and refined in parallel. Steps are refined
depending on their error (εstep ). When given a new integral, PadaQuad will simulta-
neously evaluate integration steps based on a randomly sampled grid, shown as black
points in Fig. 1b. Regions with an error larger than the tolerance will be split in
half, and new evaluation points will be made and evaluated in parallel (gray points in
Fig. 1b). This procedure continues until all integration steps satisfy the error criteria
(white points in Fig. 1b) or have been split a maximum number of times (5 by default;
Algorithm 4). The error criteria

                              εstep < Max(atol , rtol × L′ ),                       (E59)

where atol and rtol are the absolute and relative tolerances, and L′ is the latest path
integral value. The atol and rtol values determine the integrators accuracy. To evaluate
an integration step’s error Eq. E59, the integral (L′ ) must be known. PadaQuad defines
L′ as the integral with the current integration steps. As the integration steps are
refined, L′ is updated with each parallel evaluation. Thus, the integral value and the
grid refinement converge simultaneously.
    To further increase speed when integrating the same function, PadaQuad uses
the previous evaluation points as an initial evaluation for the next optimization step.
PadaQuad avoids evaluating excess temporal points by pruning the evaluation time


                                            41
points at the end of each integration. Note that PadaQuad uses all evaluated inte-
gration steps in the returned integral value; this pruned grid is only used to initialize
the integration steps in the next optimization. When initializing the integral with the
previous integration times, PadaQuad generally requires 2 or 3 adaptive evaluations
in the systems we tested.
    Popcornn is designed to handle systems that require large memory budgets,
which may stem from expensive potentials or from a many-atom system. To do this,
PadaQuad calculates the maximum number of evaluations that can fit onto the GPU,
and as a redundancy, reduces the evaluation batch size on out-of-memory errors.
PadaQuad can reduce the batch size to a single evaluation, thus handling any system
where a single evaluation can fit within the GPU’s memory. The user may also set the
evaluation batch size themselves to optimize Popcornn on their own hardware.

G.1     A Complete Popcornn Run
A Popcornn run is a sequence of optimization stages that share the path parameters
θ. For the molecular benchmarks in this work there are two stages, initial path con-
struction and path optimization on the MLIP (Methods), each with its own potential,
integrand, and Adam optimizer. Every optimizer iteration integrates the gradient of
the path loss along the whole path (Algorithm 3) and updates all of θ at once. After
the last iteration of a stage, one more integration records dE/dt along the path, from
which the TS is located (Algorithm 5); the TS that Popcornn reports is the one found
after the final stage. Optionally, the loss integral and the TS search are also computed
at every iteration for monitoring; they do not change θ.

G.2     Evaluating the Path
Algorithm 2 evaluates the path model of Eq. 2 at a batch of reaction times, with
the network input rescaled to t̃ = 2t − 1 (Supplementary Note A.3). All quantities
are computed for the whole batch at once and stay differentiable with respect to
θ. The velocity vθ (t) is the derivative of the path with respect to t, obtained by
automatic differentiation rather than by finite differences. The potential is called once
per batch; the repository provides one wrapper per supported MLIP that returns
energies and atomic forces for a batch of structures. For the force-based losses, the
gradient with respect to θ passes through the forces, so it needs the derivative of the
forces with respect to the atomic positions. For models whose forces are the negative
energy gradient, this derivative is the negative of the PES Hessian (the matrix of
second derivatives of the energy), and automatic differentiation applies it to vectors
without forming the full matrix. For models that predict forces directly, or for the
analytic forces of the pair potential, automatic differentiation differentiates that force
expression instead.

G.5     Locating the Transition State
Algorithm 5 is the full TS search summarized in Methods. It uses the samples recorded
during the final integration of a stage and brackets each zero of dE/dt between neigh-
bouring samples. Because each integration step is evaluated at its two ends, the first
and last samples lie exactly at the reactant and product (t = 0 and t = 1). These
two samples are excluded from the brackets because, at and close to the reactant and
product minima, dE/dt is near zero and its sign is unreliable. The reactant and prod-
uct times are always candidates, so that a path without an interior maximum returns
the higher-energy endpoint. Taking the highest-energy candidate also discards zeros
of dE/dt that are energy minima.
